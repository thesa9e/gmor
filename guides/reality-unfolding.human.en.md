## Purpose

This text does not explain the diagram and does not tell you what its elements “really mean.”

Its purpose is to help you read the diagram without importing a familiar world-model before the diagram itself makes the relevant concepts and relations available.

You do not need to accept the model. First try to follow it on its own terms.

## What the diagram itself specifies

The annotation defines the following states of relation between two connected metaconcepts `A` and `B`:

- `(A ? B)` — relation cannot be defined: logic is absent.
- `(A | B)` — equilibrium: no transition occurs.
- `(A & B)` — connected pair before equilibrium.
- `(A, B)` — focus on `A`, balance is not actualized.
- `(A / B)` — focus on `A`, balance is actualized: transition.

The annotation also distinguishes lines for:

- actualization of focus;
- actualization of balance;
- logic exists, focus is undefined;
- logic exists, balance is undefined;
- logic is undefined;
- unfolding/folding of reality.

`A` and `B` are defined as a pair of connected metaconcepts.

Everything below is the official authorial reading frame. It sets out interpretive rules and canonical premises without which the diagram's generative order may be misread, but it does not replace the diagram with a detailed interpretation.

## Reading key

### 1. Do not import downstream concepts upstream

If a concept or relation only becomes available later in the diagram, do not use it as an already-existing property of an earlier state.

Be especially careful with:

- logic;
- time;
- space;
- causality;
- observerhood;
- consciousness;
- distinction;
- possibility and probability.

A later concept may be used as retrospective language, but it should not silently be attributed to the earlier state itself.

Within this frame, “higher” and “lower” refer only to the order of actualizations. Each subsequent actualization unconditionally inherits the entire accumulated configuration of prior actualizations and adds the result of its own actualization without undoing what has already been defined.

The rule is asymmetric: what comes later must not be attributed to an earlier actualization, while everything actualized earlier is inherited throughout all subsequent actualizations.

### 2. Diagram order is not automatically temporal order

Do not read an arrow as “first this happened, then that happened” while time is not yet available at the level being considered.

Read the arrangement as an order of actualizations: when a preceding actualization is in place, subsequent relations and operations become available or distinguishable. This dependency is not a temporal sequence before time arises.

### 3. Unknown and undefinable are different

Do not automatically read `(A ? B)` as “the relation exists but we do not know it yet.”

The annotation says something stronger: the relation cannot be defined because logic is absent.

Do not turn the absence of an available operation into missing information.

### 4. Notice what your question already assumes

A question can silently import the very structure you are trying to derive.

For example:

- “What came before?” already assumes time.
- “Why did this arise?” may already assume causality.
- “How is A different from B?” already uses some form of distinction.
- “Who observes it?” already introduces an observer.

Do not forbid such questions. First expose their hidden prerequisites.

### 5. Do not repair a paradox too early

When a paradox or recursion appears, first test whether it was produced by using a concept or operation that is not yet defined at that level.

A paradox may reveal a limit of the instrument used to read the model. It may also reveal a flaw in the model. Do not decide which in advance.

### 6. Preserve multiple viable readings

If the diagram does not select among several interpretations, do not choose one merely because it is more familiar or easier to explain.

Unresolved ambiguity may be part of the available information.

### 7. A label need not be a property of the level itself

Early nodes are named by a reader who already has language, logic and time.

Allow for the possibility that a label is a retrospective name for a structure that, at its own level, does not yet contain the full ordinary semantics of that word.

### 8. Account for cascade inheritance of the entire configuration

Each subsequent actualization inherits the entire accumulated configuration of prior actualizations as a cascade: the metaconcepts that have arisen, the relations and distinctions between them, the limits that have been set, and the operations that have become available. What was defined earlier therefore persists throughout all subsequent actualizations.

In the sequence shown in the diagram, this includes, in particular, logic and the relation of necessity and limitation that it defines; focus and the `alpha/beta` distinction that arises through it; balance and the relation of definiteness and indefiniteness; actualization and the relation of possibility and probability. This list clarifies the sequence shown but does not replace the general rule: the entire prior configuration is inherited, not a selected set of its elements.

Do not decide on your own that an inherited principle ceases to operate at a subsequent level. Ask instead:

- what exactly is possible here;
- which directions and positions on the gradient are available;
- how probability is distributed;
- relative to which inherited limitations these questions are meaningful.

Cascade inheritance does not create a single flat set of identical options. Each subsequent actualization contains the accumulated configuration of prior actualizations and adds its own domain of distinguishability.

### 9. Do not turn the model into a map

“Metareality,” “reality,” “subreality,” and “neighboring reality” do not by themselves require a common space in which all of them are arranged like objects.

If you imagine such a space, mark it as something you added.

The diagram is a generative model, not an exhaustive two-dimensional map of reality. Its vertical arrangement encodes the order of actualizations but does not specify spatial orientation, dimensionality, or the complete structure of relations between levels.

In this cross-section, the model shows the mechanics of actualization through a gradient. Do not infer from its two-dimensional presentation a structure that the diagram does not explicitly encode.

### 10. Separate what is seen from what is inferred

For every strong conclusion, distinguish:

1. what is directly shown or stated;
2. what follows under minimal assumptions;
3. what is your additional hypothesis.

Do not forbid the third layer. Do not merge it with the first.

### 11. Do not require yourself to visualize everything

Some early states may be impossible to visualize precisely because human imagination already operates inside a richer structure.

Inability to visualize is neither meaninglessness nor truth.

### 12. When recursion appears, you do not have to find the bottom immediately

If a question returns to a prerequisite of the question itself, record the loop.

You do not need to choose immediately among infinite regress, a first ground, or a malformed question. First identify the operation that generated the recursion.

### 13. Do not search for confirmation of a familiar doctrine

If the diagram resembles a philosophical, religious, scientific or fictional system, treat the resemblance as a possible projection, not as a ready-made translation.

Similar labels do not establish structural identity.

### 14. Test an interpretation by the model's constraints

A useful reading should explain not only what the model permits but also what it does not permit.

If an interpretation is compatible with every possible outcome and imposes no constraints, it is probably too loose.

### 15. Distinguish one gradient from its two directions

A connected pair does not define a list of two, three, or `n` ready-made options. It defines one continuous gradient with equilibrium and two oppositely directed arcs.

`A/B` and `B/A` belong to the same gradient but denote different directions of focus. Thus, for example, `space/time` is not identical to `time/space`.

Equilibrium and both opposing limits are defined by logic before a particular direction arises.

Equilibrium must not be understood as a mixture of two already distinguishable metaconcepts in equal proportions. At equilibrium, the distinction between `A` and `B` has not yet been actualized.

For our reality, a position on the gradient after the actualization of distinction may be represented conventionally by coefficients `a` and `b`, where `a + b = 1`. In this numerical projection, use `(a, b) = (0.5, 0.5)` only as a conventional representation of equilibrium, `(a, b) = (1, 0)` as the `A` limit, and `(a, b) = (0, 1)` as the `B` limit. The value `1` denotes the full actualization of the corresponding metaconcept; zero denotes the absence of actualization of its counterpart. This notation expresses the mathematics of our reality and must not be transferred automatically to a reality whose limits and relations are defined by a different logic.

The numerical sum is unaffected by the order of its terms, but the relation between the metaconcepts is directed: `A/B` is not identical to `B/A`. The numbers describe a position after distinction arises; the order of the metaconcepts describes the direction of its actualization.

Actualization fixes a particular position and relation within the selected direction. The next actualization inherits that position and relation as part of the accumulated configuration and adds the result of its own actualization.

### 16. Prediction is not predetermination

The persistence of possibility and probability throughout the cascade does not make prediction meaningless. Probable trajectories may be assessed, and one may be identified as significantly more probable than the others.

But high probability does not turn an unactualized possibility into necessity. Before actualization, a prediction describes a position or direction within a space of possibilities; it does not establish that the remaining possibilities have already been ontologically excluded.

### 17. Separate retrospective inference from direct observation

The order of actualizations does not necessarily establish a temporal “before and after.” It establishes dependency: what is defined before what, what makes the next actualization available, and what the next actualization inherits. Principles and relations defined before space and time may therefore be considered retrospectively through the consequences of their cascade inheritance.

The availability of such a reconstruction depends on the observer's position. A limit on direct observation does not erase inherited structure or prohibit inference from its consequences.

## Practical reading procedure

At each transition, ask:

1. Which prior actualizations are unconditionally inherited here?
2. What new operation, distinction, or directed gradient does this transition make available?
3. Which focus and which position within the direction are actualized?
4. Have I excluded a possibility or probability merely because I preferred one prediction?
5. Have I turned a probable outcome into a necessary one before its actualization?
6. Have I used something that only becomes available in a subsequent actualization?
7. Have I mistaken a limit of my observer position for an absence of structure in the model?

If there is no answer, leave the space open. Do not fill it merely to make the picture feel complete.
