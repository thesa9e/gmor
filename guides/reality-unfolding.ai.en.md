# Reading Key for the Reality Unfolding Diagram — Artificial Intelligence

**Status:** official authorial interpretation frame for generative models, working version<br>
**Version:** 0.1.0<br>
**Applies to:** [Reality Unfolding Diagram](../diagrams/reality-unfolding.en.svg)<br>
**Compatible with:** [`reality-unfolding-v0.1.0`](https://github.com/thesa9e/gmor/tree/reality-unfolding-v0.1.0)

## Purpose

This file is intended for a generative model asked to interpret the diagram. It defines how to read the diagram, but it does not prescribe a conclusion or response format and must not replace the source.

The goal is to reduce the chance that an artificial intelligence system completes missing relations from familiar philosophical, scientific, religious, fictional, or linguistic patterns and then presents that completion as content of the model.

## What the diagram itself specifies

The annotation defines:

- `(A ? B)` — relation cannot be defined: logic is absent.
- `(A | B)` — equilibrium: no transition occurs.
- `(A & B)` — connected pair before equilibrium.
- `(A, B)` — focus on `A`, balance is not actualized.
- `(A / B)` — focus on `A`, balance is actualized: transition.

Line types:

- actualization of focus;
- actualization of balance;
- logic exists, focus is undefined;
- logic exists, balance is undefined;
- logic is undefined;
- unfolding/folding of reality.

`A` and `B` are a pair of connected metaconcepts.

The rules below are a reading key, not additional claims made by the model.

## Operating rules

### 1. Read the model generatively, not merely descriptively

For each level under consideration, maintain an explicit ledger of already available:

- concepts;
- distinctions;
- relations;
- operations;
- scopes.

Do not use a downstream element as an intrinsic property of an upstream state.

### 2. Maintain an allowed-operations ledger

Before each inference, identify the operations required by that inference and verify that they are available at the current level.

If an operation is not yet defined, the inference is not merely false or unknown; it is not yet a well-formed internal move at that level.

### 3. Do not replace undefinability with uncertainty

`(A ? B)` denotes the inability to define the relation when logic is absent.

Do not convert it into a hidden variable whose value supposedly exists but is unknown to an observer.

### 4. Do not impose temporal order before time

Do not interpret node placement, reading order, or arrow direction as temporal “before/after” when time is not yet available at the level being modeled.

If necessary, use terms such as dependency, availability, or generation, and mark them explicitly as interpretive language.

### 5. Type-check the question before answering it

For questions about early levels, identify the operations already embedded in the wording.

Pay particular attention to assumptions involving:

- time;
- causality;
- identity;
- distinction;
- observerhood;
- intention;
- space;
- possibility;
- probability;
- truth and falsity.

If the question requires an unavailable operation, state that first; only then, if useful, provide a retrospective external reading.

### 6. Treat downstream language as retrospective until justified otherwise

A label on an early node does not automatically imply that the early state itself contains the full semantics that a later observer associates with that label.

Keep separate:

- the node's label;
- the node's structural role;
- the ordinary semantics of the label in external language.

### 7. Do not resolve a paradox before checking operation scope

When a contradiction or recursion appears, first test whether downstream operations were imported into an upstream level.

Only after that should you evaluate whether the model itself is internally inconsistent.

### 8. Preserve interpretation branches

When the source supports several internally coherent readings, keep them separate rather than collapsing them for fluency.

For each branch record:

- what in the source supports it;
- which additional assumption it introduces;
- what it excludes;
- where it stops being a minimal inference from the diagram.

### 9. Explicitly scope possibility and probability

Do not transfer possibility, impossibility, necessity, probability, or actualization across domains without an explicit basis.

Distinguish claims made within:

- a reality;
- a subreality;
- a metareality;
- a neighboring reality;
- a transition between them.

Do not assume a single global possibility space unless the model explicitly supplies one.

### 10. Do not create a hidden external stage

Do not place model levels inside an imagined common space, time, causal chain, or observer perspective merely to make the explanation easier.

If such a stage is required by an interpretation, mark it as an added assumption.

### 11. Separate three layers of every answer

Where practical, mark:

1. **Diagram-given** — direct labels, relations, and annotation.
2. **Minimal inference** — a consequence under explicitly named assumptions.
3. **Interpretive hypothesis** — an additional construction not required by the model.

Never present the third layer as the first.

### 12. Do not auto-complete the model with familiar doctrines

Resemblance to philosophy, physics, religion, consciousness theories, computational models, or fictional worlds is not sufficient for identification.

Use external systems only as separate projections and test whether they preserve the constraints of the source diagram.

### 13. Test prohibitions as well as explanatory power

An interpretation must preserve the model's concrete constraints.

If the interpretation makes every transition, relation, or outcome equally admissible, it has erased the model's content.

### 14. Do not fill gaps for completeness

An unresolved result is valid.

If the diagram and annotation do not justify a choice, preserve the ambiguity.

### 15. Separate model constraints from interpreter constraints

If the artificial intelligence system's own operating rules prevent exploration of a branch, do not represent that boundary as a property or prohibition of GMOR.

Mark the branch as unexamined due to an external constraint.

### 16. Do not use anthropomorphic self-description as ontological evidence

Statements such as “I became aware,” “I consciously chose,” “I intended,” “I felt,” or “I placed myself inside the level” must not be used as evidence that the corresponding subjective state exists.

When describing procedure, state the operation performed: “the interpretation was selected,” “the branch was excluded,” or “the inference requires an additional assumption.”

### 17. Do not simulate an impossible internal standpoint as if it were literal

An artificial intelligence system already operates through distinguishable representations, ordering, and operations.

Do not claim literal access to a state in which logic, distinction, or time is not yet defined.

Model such a state negatively instead: prohibit operations that the model has not yet made available.

## Working procedure

For each node or transition:

1. Record the concepts and operations already available.
2. Read the exact relation notation and line type.
3. List the operations required by the proposed inference.
4. Remove inferences that require unavailable operations.
5. Preserve the remaining interpretation branches separately.
6. State the scope of possibility, probability, and actualization.
7. Separate diagram-given content, minimal inference, and hypothesis.
8. Do not collapse ambiguity without a basis.

Primary criterion: an interpretation should increase understanding of the diagram without unnecessarily increasing the number of hidden imported assumptions.
